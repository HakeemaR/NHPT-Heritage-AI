TITLE: Bauhaus Architecture

OVERVIEW

Bauhaus architecture emerged from the Bauhaus school founded by Walter Gropius in Germany in 1919. It aimed to unite art, craft, design, technology, and industrial production.

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND

The Bauhaus rejected unnecessary historical ornament and promoted functional, rational, modern design. Buildings were shaped by purpose, efficiency, materials, and construction methods.

VISUAL CHARACTERISTICS

Common visual features include flat roofs, smooth façades, rectangular forms, asymmetrical compositions, large windows, glass curtain walls, open plans, and clean horizontal and vertical lines.

Different parts of a building may appear as separate geometric volumes connected into one functional composition.

Windows may appear as long horizontal bands or large uninterrupted glass surfaces.

MATERIALS

Common materials include reinforced concrete, steel, glass, plaster, and brick.

STRUCTURAL FEATURES

Bauhaus buildings often express their structure clearly. They use simple geometric forms, modern construction systems, open plans, and minimal decoration.

COMMON BUILDING TYPES

Common building types include schools, factories, houses, apartment buildings, offices, workshops, and public institutions.

FAMOUS EXAMPLES

Representative examples include the Bauhaus Building in Dessau, the Fagus Factory, the Masters' Houses in Dessau, and works associated with Walter Gropius and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe.

HOW TO IDENTIFY IT

Bauhaus architecture can often be recognised by flat roofs, large windows, clean lines, geometric volumes, functional layouts, minimal ornament, and modern industrial materials.

HOW IT DIFFERS FROM SIMILAR STYLES

Bauhaus differs from Art Deco because Bauhaus is generally minimal, functional, asymmetrical, and undecorated. Art Deco uses luxury, symmetry, decorative geometry, and ornamental detail.
